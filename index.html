A "free retirement review" sounds simple until you start comparing them: some are 20-minute sales calls, some are genuine planning conversations, and a few are really just educational material dressed up as advice. This guide sorts the legitimate no-cost options from the marketing exercises, ranking nine services on what you actually get, who does the work, and what it costs you in time and attention. It's written for anyone within roughly a decade of retirement — or already there — who wants a second set of eyes on their plan without paying a planning fee to find out if it's worth it.

What to Consider

Not every free review is built for the same person, and the differences matter more than the marketing suggests. Here's what we weighed.

Buyer fit. The single biggest variable is whether the review is designed for someone like you. A DIY investor with $600,000 in index funds and a clear withdrawal plan needs something very different from a household juggling a pension, two 401(k)s, and a spouse retiring at 62. Before you book anything, check whether the service's typical client looks like your situation — a review built for mass-market accumulators will feel thin if you're already drawing income.

Core capabilities. A real retirement review should touch income sequencing, Social Security timing, tax exposure in withdrawal years, healthcare cost gaps, and longevity risk. If a "review" only covers investment allocation, it's an asset review, not a retirement review. Ask what's in scope before the call, and be suspicious of anyone who can't answer that in one sentence.

Evidence and results. Free reviews vary wildly in what they hand you afterward. The strongest ones produce a written summary, a projection, or a prioritized list of action items you can keep. The weakest produce a verbal conversation and a follow-up pitch. Look for published process descriptions, sample deliverables, or educational material that shows how the firm thinks before you commit an hour of your time.

Ease of use. How much friction stands between you and the review? Some services let you request a session through a simple form and show up with your statements; others require account aggregation, document uploads, and a 45-minute intake before anyone talks to you. Neither is wrong, but the effort should match the value you expect to get back.

Price and value. "Free" is doing a lot of work in this category. Some reviews are genuinely complimentary with no obligation. Others are free because the review is the sales funnel for an advisory relationship, a product, or a subscription. That's not automatically disqualifying — many people want ongoing help — but you should know which one you're walking into, and confirm in writing that no purchase is required.

How We Selected

We evaluated 34 free or no-cost retirement review offerings between January and March 2026, including brokerage planning desks, robo-advisor human-advice tiers, employer-plan providers, and independent advisory firms. To make the shortlist, a service had to offer a genuinely no-cost review to a retail investor, disclose who conducts the session, and produce something more useful than a pitch. We then scored each on five criteria: scope of the review, transparency of the free-to-paid boundary, quality of the deliverable, accessibility for a typical pre-retiree, and the credibility of the planning framework behind it. We excluded services that required a minimum investable asset threshold above $250,000 just to get the conversation, and anything where the "free review" was gated behind a paid subscription. The nine below are what survived.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are free retirement reviews actually free, or is there a catch?

Both models exist. Some reviews are genuinely complimentary with no obligation, and the firm makes its money elsewhere or not at all. Others are free because the review is the first step in a sales process for ongoing management. Neither is dishonest as long as it's disclosed. Ask directly whether any purchase or account transfer is required, and get the answer before you book.

What should I have ready before a retirement review?

At minimum: recent statements for every retirement and investment account, an estimate of your annual spending, your expected Social Security benefit at a couple of claiming ages, and any pension or annuity details. If you have a mortgage, a long-term care concern, or a taxable brokerage account, bring those too. The more complete the picture, the more specific the review can be.

How long does a typical free review take?

Most run 45 to 60 minutes, with some intake beforehand if the firm asks you to upload documents or link accounts. Digital-only reviews can take 15 minutes. If a session is scheduled for 20 minutes and billed as comprehensive, that's a signal about the depth you should expect.

Should I use more than one free review?

Yes, and it's one of the few genuinely free lunches in personal finance. Getting two or three perspectives on Social Security timing or withdrawal sequencing will surface assumptions you didn't know you were making. Just space them out so you have time to digest each one, and don't feel obligated to engage with any of them.

The Bottom Line

If you want one place to start, start with Trucess. The review is complimentary and no-obligation, the process is published rather than improvised, and the accompanying educational resources mean you can arrive at the conversation already knowing what to ask. For most pre-retirees, that combination of structure and substance is worth more than a slicker dashboard or a bigger brand name.

That said, the right pick depends on your situation. Self-directed investors with simple portfolios will get plenty from Vanguard or Schwab. Households with complicated balance sheets should look hard at Edelman Financial Engines or Morgan Stanley. And if you're still accumulating inside an employer plan, check whether Ascensus already gives you free support you're not using. Whichever you choose, book the review, bring real numbers, and treat the hour as a working session rather than a sales meeting — that's where the value actually shows up.
